One Login, Many Teachers

Home Blog One Login, Many Teachers

September 2026 · Abdelrahman Khalawy

The Student Management Platform is a multi-tenant SaaS for private tutoring — teacher, student, and parent portals across web and mobile, built for a market where a single student rarely studies with just one teacher. That last detail turned out to shape the entire identity model.

The tenant boundary that doesn't fit

In most multi-tenant products, "tenant" is a clean boundary: one company, one workspace, one set of users who belong to it and nowhere else. That model breaks down here. Each teacher on the platform is effectively their own tenant — their own students, schedule, materials, and payments. But a real student studies with several teachers across several subjects. If tenancy is modeled the obvious way, that student needs a separate account, and a separate login, for every teacher. That's not a minor inconvenience; it's the kind of friction that makes a platform feel broken before a user even reaches the features.

A unified identity, scoped per relationship

The design I settled on treats identity and tenancy as separate concerns. A student has one account and one login — full stop. What changes per teacher isn't the account, it's the relationship: which teacher, which subject, which data that relationship is allowed to see. Logging in resolves the person; entering a specific teacher's space resolves the relationship, and the data available is scoped to that relationship, not to the account as a whole.

Two-filter isolation

Enforcing that safely needs more than the single tenant-scoping filter I'd use on a conventional multi-tenant system (the kind SehhaTech relies on for per-clinic isolation). Here, isolation has to hold on two axes at once:

  • Teacher scoping — data belongs to a specific teacher's space and must never bleed into another's, the same guarantee a conventional tenant filter gives.
  • Relationship scoping — within a teacher's space, a student or parent can only see the data tied to their own relationship with that teacher, not every student that teacher has.

Both filters apply together, automatically, the same way a single tenant filter would on a simpler system — no call site gets to apply just one and assume the other is implied. A query that's correctly scoped to a teacher but forgets the relationship boundary is exactly as much of a bug as one that ignores tenancy altogether, so both have to be enforced at the same infrastructure level, not left to be remembered case by case.

What this actually buys the user

From a student's side, the result is simple: one account, one login, and every teacher they study with shows up inside it — switching between a math teacher and a physics teacher feels like switching tabs, not logging into two different products. From a teacher's side, nothing changes: their space is exactly as isolated as it would be on a single-tenant system, because the relationship-level filter never lets another teacher's view leak in.

Leading this as a team decision

I lead an 8-person engineering team on this platform, and this identity model is the kind of decision that has to be made once and communicated clearly — every portal (teacher, student, parent, web, mobile) has to agree on what "logged in" and "scoped to this teacher" actually mean. That's exactly the kind of decision that belongs in a written spec and a decision log, not tribal knowledge — the same discipline I wrote about in spec-driven development with Claude Code, applied to a design decision instead of a single feature.
